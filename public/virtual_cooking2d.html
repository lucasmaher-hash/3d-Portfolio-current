Overview / Concept

0

Cooking with a recipe rarely goes smoothly — you're constantly wiping your hands to check a measurement, flip back a page, or squint at a timer across the kitchen. Virtual Cooking imagines a VR headset that keeps all of that in view instead: instructions on the left, ingredients and running timers on the right, hands free the entire time.

Most VR design leans safe and sterile. I wanted to push in the other direction — a distinct, tactile visual language built from 3D panels rather than flat menus, finished in a shiny brushed-silver material as a small homage to the stainless steel that fills every real kitchen.

Instructions, Without Lifting a Finger

1

The left panel holds the recipe itself — steps, quantities, technique notes — always sitting in the corner of your vision. Swipe through it the same way you'd flip a page, no controller, no sticky fingers on a screen.

Ingredients & Timers, Always in Reach

2

The right panel tracks what's cooking. Ingredient lists stay pinned next to the pan they belong to, and timers run alongside them — set with a glance, checked without turning around.

No Controllers Needed

3

Every interaction — opening a step, setting a timer, dismissing a panel — works through hand tracking alone: a click to select, a swipe to move on. The goal was an interface that disappears into the act of cooking rather than adding another device to hold.

Reality Check

A Concept, Not (Yet) a Product

This is a concept, not a working prototype. Consumer VR headsets today are still bulky, heat up fast, and aren't rated for splashes, heat, or flour — hardware built for gaming, not a stovetop. Cooking is also a deeply tactile, social activity, and strapping on a headset to make dinner would trade away most of what makes it enjoyable at home.

Where the idea gets more interesting is outside the home kitchen.

A Better Fit

Where This Could Work Today

Professional kitchens are a different story. A busy service means a cook juggling a dozen tickets, each with its own recipe, timing, and plating spec — usually tracked on paper or a screen bolted to the wall. A heads-up display that surfaces the right recipe and running timer for whatever's on the pass in front of you, hands-free, in a loud and fast-moving line, is a genuinely useful problem to solve — and one lightweight AR glasses, rather than a full VR headset, could actually take on as the hardware matures.
